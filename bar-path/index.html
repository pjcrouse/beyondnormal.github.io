How accurate is it?

We measured. Here's the paperwork.

Every velocity product says “accurate.” None of them publish what that means. So we dropped a loaded barbell fifteen times and measured gravity with the same code that measures your set — because gravity is the one number whose right answer you already know.

1.25%
Repeatability

Combined standard uncertainty across fifteen measurements. The target was 2%, set before any data existed.

3.00%
From standard gravity

With no correction of any kind applied. It reproduces to ±0.46%, which makes it a property of the setup rather than the method.

None
Effect on velocity loss

Loss is a ratio measured through one ruler, so a constant offset divides out exactly — it cannot reach the number above.

The full report is public, including the parts that went badly: nine defects found and fixed, a hypothesis that failed its own test, and a clause listing what we still haven’t established.

Why it matters

AMRAP reps are the one number you self-report.

Everything else in 5/3/1 is prescribed. Your last set is the exception — you decide when to rack it, and that decision drives your training max for the next cycle. Bar speed is the receipt. A set that ends at 15% velocity loss and a set that ends at 40% are two different sessions, no matter what you wrote in the log.

Did you actually go to failure?

If your bar speed barely drops across the set, you stopped early — and your training max is about to move on a number that wasn't real. If it falls off a cliff, you emptied the tank and the PR is honest.

Where does the bar leave the line?

Drift shows the rep where the pull started drifting forward, and by how much. Not a coach's opinion — the actual path the bar took, traced from your own video.

How to film it

Three things to get right.

Set the phone side-on. Lean it against a water bottle if you have to. Keep the whole plate, the bar, and the floor in frame for the entire set, and shoot somewhere reasonably lit. Side-on matters more than anything else — the tool measures a two-dimensional path, so an angled camera measures an angled path.

Film your top set. The heavy one taken near failure is the one worth measuring. That's where speed drops off and where form tends to come apart — a set of easy doubles won't tell you much.

Trim to your working reps. Drag the two markers so they cover the reps that count. Cut the walk-up, the setup, the chalk, and any failed rep at the end. Everything between the markers gets tracked, rep by rep.
